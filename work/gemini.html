Google Gemini Smart Context

A redesign of the Google Gemini home screen for iOS. I rebuilt the existing app screen by screen so I was working against the real thing, watched students open it, and replaced the generic prompt row with two suggestion columns that read the time of day and the work already in progress.

Overview

The app knows a great deal and opens with none of it

Gemini can hold a long technical conversation, read a photograph, and write working code. Then you open it and get your first name, a text field, and a row of suggested prompts that would look identical on anyone else's phone. The assistant is genuinely capable. The screen you meet first uses none of that capability.

This project is a redesign of that screen. I recreated the existing app end to end before changing anything, so that the comparison was against the real product rather than my memory of it, and the recreation is what made the gap on the home screen obvious.

Research

What people do in the first ten seconds

I spoke with classmates who use an AI assistant most days and asked them to open the app in front of me rather than describe how they use it. Watching the first ten seconds turned out to be more useful than anything they said about features afterwards.

The behaviour was consistent enough to design against. Almost nobody read the suggested prompts. People either went straight to the text field and typed, or went into the chat history to find a conversation they had already started. The suggestions occupied the most valuable space on the screen and were being scrolled past on the way to somewhere else.

01

The prompts get skipped

The suggestions are general enough that reading them costs more attention than typing the question already in your head.

02

Most opens are continuations

People came back to a thread they had already started, but resuming one meant remembering it existed and then going to look for it.

03

The context is there and unused

The app already knows the hour, the location, and the last conversation. None of that reaches the first screen.

That is what moved the project to the home screen rather than the chat. The conversation itself works. The problem sits in the ten seconds before it starts.

The Redesign

Two columns answering two different questions

The greeting stays. Underneath it, instead of one row of generic prompts, there are two labelled columns. The left column reads the moment, meaning the time of day and where you are. The right column picks up work already in progress. Each column holds a small stack of cards and cycles through them, so the screen has something different to say at eleven at night than it does on a Tuesday morning.

Drag a card sideways, or use the dots. The two columns run on their own timing, the way they do on the phone.

Both columns had to earn the space they took from the text field, so each card carries a reason rather than a category. The evening card does not say "restaurants near you", it names Shibam Coffee on Centre Avenue and tells you it closes at eleven. The work card does not say "recent chats", it names the Python thread and the specific thing left to learn in it. A suggestion is only worth reading if it is more specific than the question you were already going to type.

The columns move independently

These three states are the same screen at three moments. The evening column moves from late-night coffee to dinner nearby while the work column stays on the Python thread, and then the work column moves on to the study app project with its open questions. Neither column waits for the other, which keeps the screen from feeling like a slideshow with one hand on the controls.

The Recreation

Rebuilding the app before changing it

Before designing anything I recreated the existing app, from signing in through to rating an answer. Redrawing an interface at full size is the quickest way to find out where it is thin, and it meant every later decision was argued against the real product instead of a screenshot in my head.

The flow below runs left to right in the order a session actually happens, with the redesigned home screen sitting in its place so the whole thing reads as one product rather than a rebuild and a proposal kept apart. It follows a single thread: a beginner asks what a Python if statement does, gets a structured answer with working code, and then tells the assistant the formatting was wrong. I kept the screens that carry the argument and left out the ones that only showed a stock pattern working normally.

Reflections

Rebuilding the app first was the most useful decision in the project. I had opinions about Gemini's interface before I started, and drawing the screens at full size is what sorted the ones that held up from the ones that were really just unfamiliarity.

Watching people open the app taught me more than asking them about it did. What someone says they want from a home screen and what they do in the first five seconds are not the same thing, and only one of those is worth designing for.

If I kept going I would work on what happens when the context is wrong. A suggestion that misreads the moment is worse than no suggestion at all, and I never designed the case where a card is simply unhelpful, or how someone would say so and have the screen learn from it.
