The Brief

An app feature to keep shoppers in the store longer

The brief asked for an app-first feature that keeps customers in the store longer, measured in dollars: basket size, return visits, conversion and fewer walk-outs. It came with a prototype, ExtraTime, and six earlier initiatives not to repeat. Read against the analytics package, it had three problems:

Time in the store was already up

Dwell rose 14% after the endcaps moved, and abandonment rose two points with it.

App-first leaves out the most loyal shoppers

Seniors, the most loyal shoppers, made 4% of in-app coupon redemptions.

Nudges to stay had backfired

The two-hour ExtraBucks timer drew the sharpest survey comments and a $47,500 settlement.

Research

What the region had already tried

The brief came with Region 4's analytics package: thirty months of metrics from eight Pittsburgh stores, six earlier initiatives and their results, coupon redemptions by age, and a sample of survey comments. I read it for what had already been tried, and how shoppers answered.

Data insights

Paper still carries the oldest shoppers

Shoppers 65 and over made 39% of printed coupon redemptions, the largest share of any age group.

An extra step at checkout fails

Clipped coupons rang up at full price and held up the line; the button gave no sign it had worked.

Information at the shelf worked, screens that needed staff did not

The aisle locator scored 4.7 with its users. The kiosk needed a staff member for 61% of its sessions.

Takeaway

Whatever replaced the brief had to work for people who never open the app, add no step at checkout, and not rush anyone.

Source: CVS Region 4 analytics package provided with the brief, covering January 2024 to June 2026 across eight stores. Survey comments are the package's stratified sample, about 2% of responses.

The Reframe

From more time in the store to money already set aside

A health FSA is pre-tax money for medical costs, and it expires. In 2022, half of accountholders forfeited part of theirs, $441 on average. CVS sells what that money is for, so I changed the question: from keeping people in the store to helping them spend what they already have before it is gone.

From

"How might the app keep customers in the store longer?"

To

"How might the app help people spend their FSA here before it expires?"

Three facts about FSA money shaped the design

It expires

Money left at year end is lost, beyond a carryover capped at $680 for 2026.

Eligibility is item by item

Pain relievers count. A chocolate bar in the same basket does not.

The split comes at checkout

Shoppers see the eligible total only when they pay.

Sources: EBRI analysis of 3.2 million FSAs, reported May 2024; IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32, section .15; OPM on the CARES Act changes; Inventory Information Approval System.

The Opportunity

The register already knows the answer

Chain pharmacies run IIAS, which tags each product eligible or not and charges the FSA the eligible total. It runs when the card is presented, so shoppers see the split only at payment.

I started by designing an eligibility checker, then realised the register already calculates the answer.

From

"Is this eligible?"

To

"$31.48 can go on your FSA"

Sources: NMI on IIAS at checkout; Flex on IIAS item checks.

Components

One kit for the app and the register

Every screen is built from components in one Figma file. The app side keeps to CVS's patterns: cards, the eligibility tag, buttons, fields and the added-to-cart sheet. The register has its own small set for the 15.6-inch customer screen: header, item row, banners and buttons.

The Register

Four ways checkout can end

Shoppers who do not use the app still pay at the register, so its customer screen shows the same split to everyone and takes one card at a time.

Future Opportunities

What reframing was worth

Asking who already had the answer changed the scope of the project.

For CVS
Forfeited FSA money is spend that already belongs to health retail, in the dollars the brief asked for. Showing the register's own split costs a screen and reaches every shopper.
For me
I learned to check what a system already knows before designing on top of it. It also put me in the conversation about what to build, which is where I want to be.

With more time

The real tokens
The prototype is set in Inter with sampled colours. I would re-skin the flow in CVS Health Sans and the actual palette.
CVS's own benefits data
The region's package has no FSA numbers, so the case rests on national averages. CVS's own attach and conversion rates would make it a real number.
Sessions with the people it is for
A WCAG self-audit checked contrast and labels. Moderated sessions with seniors and first-time FSA users would show what an automated check cannot.
Real register hardware
The gap between a card tap and a confirmed split is a timing problem, and Figma cannot stage it. I would prototype it on point-of-sale hardware.
A deadline that does something
"86 days left" states a fact. A reminder before the deadline, or a nudge toward eligible items, would help people act on it.
